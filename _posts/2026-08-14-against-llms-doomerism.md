In this age of great technological leaps and marketing hype, one has to carefully scrutinise their beliefs and opinions to preserve a little bit of mental stability. Programmers, especially, are among the most affected (although if privileged) categories, as [prosumers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosumer) of this AI/LLM revolution (which started not so long ago btw, in November 2025).

One has to remember that LLMs are just another tool in the programmer’s toolbox, even if of a very different kind, and clean up their feed from both LLM haters and LLM evangelists.

The community of programmers has been sharing its thoughts to help navigate the uncharted waters of this exciting yet troublesome transition. Reading what has popped up recently can serve as a valuable guide for the future:

- [The Value of Things, Bob Nystrom](https://web.archive.org/web/20260807094037/https://journal.stuffwithstuff.com/2026/01/24/the-value-of-things/)
- [LLMs Reward Expertise, Sean Goedecke](https://web.archive.org/web/20260809141202/https://www.seangoedecke.com/llms-reward-expertise/)
- [Don't be a meat proxy, Niklas Gruhn](https://web.archive.org/web/20260803070727/https://gruhn.me/blog/2026-08-03/)
- [Understanding is the new bottleneck, Geoffrey Litt](https://web.archive.org/web/20260815214227/https://www.geoffreylitt.com/2026/07/02/understanding-is-the-new-bottleneck)
- [“Code was never the hard part” is an insult to all programmers, Senko Rašić](https://web.archive.org/web/20260815005306/https://blog.senko.net/code-was-never-the-hard-part-is-an-insult-to-all-programmers)
- [I costi del software: far emergere ciò che il mercato non prezza, Paolo Benanti](https://web.archive.org/web/20260814124059/https://www.quotidiano.ilsole24ore.com/art.php?t=S24&e=SOLE&i=20260812&artid=2106635) (from an Italian priest!!! worth clicking on `文/A` in your browser)
- [No Man Is an Island, Fernando Borretti](https://borretti.me/article/no-man-is-an-island)

They all seem to (involutarily or not) echo some well-known voices from the past:
- [Programming as Theory Building, Peter Naur](https://pablo.rauzy.name/dev/naur1985programming.pdf)
- [Reflections on Trusting Trust, Ken Thompson](https://dl.acm.org/doi/epdf/10.1145/358198.358210)
- [Tools for Conviviality, Ivan Illich](https://www.davidtinapple.com/illich/1973_tools_for_convivality.html)

A critical view of LLMs does not downplay the scientific significance of their discovery; their success is a severe comment on how most of linguistics has ignored probabilistic approaches to the study of language (Mr. Chomsky, the eminent scholar and imaginary anarchist, is not to [blame](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-linguistics-wars-9780199740338) only for being friend with Epstein and Bannon).

Instead, it should protect people (programmers and non-programmers) from cognitively surrendering to a consensual hallucination that presents LLMs as a God-like [Logos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos_(Christianity)): an infinite wording that generates all the reality. Or a simulation that produces [simulacra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulacra_and_Simulation) in a while-true loop, if you prefer.

It’s clear that we are also in the middle of a *crisis of presence*: I borrow the term from the Italian anthropologist [Ernesto De Martino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernesto_de_Martino). It describes a profound existential moment in which an individual feels the acute risk of losing their place in history, society, and reality itself, effectively threatening their capacity to act as a human being and to transform the world through their actions.

This already happened in history. Thousands of years ago, in an [Age of Anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._R._Dodds), the Christian religion emerged, saturating the offering of meaning, order, and consolation in a time where the Roman Empire was collapsing; and it used people's spiritual needs to effectively exercise a biopolitical force.

Everyone knows (or should know) the final outcome of this story.

> And so please, stop bother us with agent [consciousness](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/29/us/anthropic-claude-morals-ai.html) when even the mind scholars don't know what it's about.

Knowing where to place our trust and how things work (do you remember the “build X from scratch/build your own X” frenzy raging just a few time ago?) remains relevant. LLMs are just a (powerful) tool to augment our [zone of proximal development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_of_proximal_development), to put it into psychological terms. But no technology is neutral. It’s up to us to make it a convivial tool that can help humanity to advance in history as a whole towards a better future.

This won't happen until LLMs are an external service that requires a $ub$cription, high electricity consumption and computing power that only high-end hardware can support. As things stand, LLMs only risk exacerbating the conflict between capital and labour (see [Will AI Crowd Out Jobs?, Paul Krugman](https://paulkrugman.substack.com/p/will-ai-crowd-out-jobs)) and increasing the power asymmetry which separates governments and corporations from civil society. Normal people (i.e. non-billionaires) will never accept the [bad things](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/oct/08/ai-death-war-sam-altman) (i.e. negative externalities) produced by this new technology. And AI CEOs would hardly grant us [fully-automated luxury communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fully_Automated_Luxury_Communism).

Code may be cheap now, but it is expensive to maintain, to trust and to build a community upon: you can generate all the [slopforks](https://github.com/malisper/pgrust) of Postgres you want, but you will fail to understand the **real** value of projects like Postgres is not its code, but its community of [contributors](https://www.postgresql.org/community/contributors/) who are devoting their life to improve and maintain its code base and documentation for the rest of their life, hopefully.
